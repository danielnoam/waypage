Slow Air About

How Oceanic Tracks Keep Planes Apart

By Mara Ellison

Somewhere over the Atlantic the cabin lights dim, and the only glow left is from the seat-back screens and a few reading lamps. Down below, nothing but water for two thousand miles. Up front, the crew are following a road that exists only on paper: the oceanic track.

Far from shore there is no radar to watch the traffic. Instead, every evening controllers on both sides of the ocean draw a fresh set of parallel tracks across it, shaped around the jet stream so that eastbound flights ride the wind and westbound ones dodge it.

Spacing by time

Without a screen showing where everyone is, separation becomes arithmetic. Each aircraft is cleared to a track, a height and a speed, and must cross each line of longitude at the minute it promised. Two planes on the same track at the same height are kept ten minutes or more apart.

That is why pilots read their position aloud so often. At every waypoint they report the time they crossed it, their height and their estimate for the next one. A controller checks the sums, and if two estimates draw too close, someone is asked to slow down by a few knots or climb to another level.

Satellites have made the gaps smaller. Many aircraft now send their position automatically every few minutes, and on the busiest tracks planes fly half the distance apart they did a decade ago. But the habit of reporting stays, because the ocean still punishes guesswork.

Five-letter waypoints

Each waypoint has a five-letter name meant to be easy to say over a crackling radio. Some are puns, some are jokes, and a few honour the people who drew the charts. Where the tracks meet land they funnel into named entry points, and the names become a kind of poetry for anyone who listens to the frequency long enough.

Between waypoints the aircraft flies a great circle, which looks curved on a flat map but is the shortest line over a globe. That is why a flight from London to New York climbs north past Ireland and brushes the edge of Greenland before turning south.

When things go wrong

If a plane has to turn back or descend in a hurry, the crew follow a set routine: turn off the track by a fixed angle, offset a few miles, and change height so they cannot meet anyone on the track they left. Lights on, radio calls on the emergency frequency, and a careful descent to a level where no one else is flying.

It rarely happens. On a normal night the hardest part of the crossing is staying awake, and the most dramatic event is the sunrise that arrives an hour early because you flew to meet it.

Arrival

When the coast appears the cabin wakes up all at once. Breakfast trays arrive, the window shades go up, and the article you were reading finally reaches its last paragraph, right where you left it.

Next time, bring a longer one. The ocean is wide and the flight is slow, and there is always one more story worth carrying with you.
